What you actually do here

Most mythology games hand you a name, a weapon and a health bar. This one hands you an egg and a god who will talk to you for as long as you keep asking.

Ask the gods their own stories

Every deity is written out by hand and given a voice. You can ask Hecate about the crossroads, or about her sisters, or about what she thinks of you — and she answers in character, from her own myth. She remembers what you said last time.

Raise what hatches

The egg becomes a Guardian. It grows through trials, through the marks the gods leave on it, and through the affinities you feed it. Two players who start the same week do not end up with the same creature.

Fits the player it has

The old stories are not gentle, and the world knows how old you are. Younger players get the myths with the harshest material held back, so a fourteen-year-old and their mother can both play without either of them getting the wrong version.

Learn the real thing

The entries are researched, not generated — sources, epithets, regional variants, the ugly parts left in. People come for a game and leave knowing which Artemis they were talking to.

I wrote all 167 of these by hand over several years, because every mythology game I tried gave me a pantheon as a list of stat blocks. The gods in those games had nothing to say. These ones do.

The AI gives the writing a voice — it does not do the writing. If that distinction matters to you, it matters to me too, and I would rather say so on the front page than have you find out later.

The gods remember you. We keep it to that.

Your Guardian, your progress and what each god remembers of you are saved to your account, so they follow you from your laptop to your phone. We keep only what the game needs to run.

What a god remembers is a short note of what you told it, kept so the next conversation picks up where the last one ended. The full back-and-forth of a conversation is not stored; only that note is.

To be exact about it: what you type does travel to the model that voices the reply, because that is how a reply gets written. It is not kept there.

Questions people actually ask

Is this the same game as the space exploration game called The Long Silence?

No. There is an unrelated space game with a similar name. This one is about mythology — 167 hand-written gods and a Gate that hands you an egg. Nothing to do with spaceships.

Can I play on my phone?

Yes, in your phone's browser. Turn it sideways — the map is drawn wide and the game asks for landscape. Nothing to install.

Do I need an account?

Not to begin. Walk up to the Gate, take the egg, start playing.

How does the AI voice the gods?

Every deity was written out by hand first — sources, epithets, regional variants, the harsh parts left in. The AI reads that writing and speaks as that god, staying in character and remembering what you told it. It voices the entries; it does not write them. It is not a person, not a spirit, and nothing it says is religious instruction or divination.

What happens to my progress if I clear my browser data?

Nothing. Your Guardian, your progress and what each god remembers of you are saved to your account, so they are still there after you clear your browser, and they follow you to any device you sign in on.

Is the content appropriate for students?

Mostly, and the parts that are not are gated. The old stories carry violence, grief and cruelty, and some traditions here are darker than others. Deities and pantheons carrying that material are marked, and opening one asks for a birthdate first. Everything else is open to anyone. The shop does not open to anyone under 18.

Can I cancel my subscription?

Any time, and everything you earned by playing stays yours. Cancelling drops you back to the free allowance of 10 messages a month; it does not take your Guardian, your progress or your unlocked pantheons.
